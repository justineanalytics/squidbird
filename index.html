Custom research example · by Justine Braun

How I proved social media algorithms were incentivizing partisanship, using tweets.

How social media sorted voters into separate bubbles before the 2016 election, and why the bubbles held even when the topic had nothing to do with politics. Originally published on Khoros.

The question

In the run-up to the 2016 election, did Twitter users actually talk across party lines, or did each side mostly talk to itself?

How I measured it

Every dot is a tweet about the 2016 election. I sorted people by the hashtags they had used at some point: blue for pro-Hillary hashtags, red for pro-Trump hashtags. Then I pulled every election-related tweet from both audiences and connected the dots wherever one person replied to or retweeted another.

The result was two dense worlds with a narrow bridge between them. Each side was mostly replying to and retweeting itself.

The follow-up

That summer's other big trending topic was Pokémon Go, which had nothing to do with politics. So I looked at the same people talking about it.

The siloing was weaker, but it was still there. Even on a topic completely outside politics, people mostly stuck to their own political sphere when deciding who to retweet, reply to, and engage with.

Why it matters for brands

Your audience isn't one crowd. It's a set of communities that rarely talk to each other, and a message that spreads in one may never reach the next. Mapping who talks to whom shows you where a campaign will travel, and where it will stall.
